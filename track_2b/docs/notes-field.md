Gleichlaut keeps the French and Italian of Switzerland's drug-reimbursement conditions in step with the German. The Federal Office of Public Health (BAG) publishes 1,688 such conditions ("limitations") in its list of reimbursed medicines, the Spezialitätenliste (SL), in three languages; each language version is binding. Review register: https://gleichlaut.mi11ion.io/ · demo video: https://gleichlaut.mi11ion.io/demo.mp4

Why it matters: in today's list, Nemluvio is reimbursed every 8 weeks in German and French and "ogni 4 settimane" in Italian; Diaphin's surcharge is 6% in German and French and 12% in Italian. A condition that says something different in each language cannot be applied consistently.

1. The audit runs today, with no model. It reads every limitation in all three languages and turns the register into 18 issues to decide, each with the three texts side by side. Two blind Claude annotators (a third on disagreements) confirm 12 findings that change a condition (Nemluvio, Diaphin, Cresemba's 16 kg limit missing in French, a French-only 62.5 kg rule in three etanercept products, Dupixent's weight tiers) and 5 minor ones; 10 of the 12 were on our own earlier census list, which the rules were built around. Experimental checks, off by default, found what nobody had listed: Amvuttra's French stop rule says an NIS increase "≤ 10" points where German and Italian say "> 10"; the Italian of Beyonttra and Vyndaqel says "on deve" (a typo for "non deve"), so a ban on combining reads as an obligation. A German phase-1 pilot pack (project mandate draft, protection-needs analysis, one case sheet per issue for the records system) is in track_2b/docs/pilot/.

2. Drafting is a review aid. When the German changes, Apertus drafts the French and Italian: the old text stays locked outside the edit, numbers never reach the model (code writes them back), and frozen deterministic checks, not a model, decide which drafts are ready and say why in the reviewer's language. The default is Apertus 8B on an ordinary CPU, fully offline. On 166 held-out amendments, with the checks frozen before the test (PREREG says which rules predate the repository), its drafts need 33.1% fewer word-level edits to reach BAG's published text than the old text with updated numbers, and the drafts marked ready carry no meaning error as read by the annotators (who caught only 5 of 17 real errors BAG had corrected, so the true count may be higher). Apertus 70B as Public AI served it (model identity not verified) saved 42.4%. On two live months the checks flagged most drafts (81.8–92.7%), so neither month met our own pre-registered rule: today a translator still reviews nearly everything, and the audit is what an office runs first.

Where it plugs in: BAG's ePL editor keeps one field per language (drafts fill French and Italian when the German changes); the applicant is asked in the triennial review whether the translations are congruent (the audit gives BAG its own check, for the whole list); translators get XLIFF for STAR Transit and a TBX termbase mined from the register; decisions export as CSV for the federal records system (GEVER).

Check us: `make run` in track_2b/ (Docker, CPU, about two minutes, no network) re-runs every recorded Apertus request from the recorded responses and re-derives every number; claims.tsv maps every number in the README, the report and these notes to a results file. eval/prereg/PREREG.md holds every pre-registered rule with an index, including the runs that failed; eval/labels/ holds every label, worklist and annotator instruction. The bounds and caveats behind each number (what the annotators can and cannot see, the 70B endpoint we do not control, which rules predate the repository) are in the project README and the 6-page report.
